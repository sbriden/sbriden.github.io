For decades, analytics teams have operated using a relatively simple model.

The business asks for a report.

The analytics team gathers requirements.

Developers build the report.

The report gets delivered.

The project is complete.

At least that's how we like to think it works.

In reality, what usually happens is something more like this:

The report gets delivered.

A week later the business wants additional filters.

A month later they need new metrics.

Three months later there are performance issues.

Six months later someone asks for a slightly different version.

A year later there are five versions of the same report, all showing slightly different numbers.

Sound familiar?

The problem isn't necessarily the report itself.

The problem is that many organizations continue treating analytics as a series of projects rather than products.

And as data becomes increasingly critical to business operations, that mindset becomes more difficult to sustain.

This is where Data Product Thinking comes into play.

# The Shift From Projects to Products

Let's start with a simple question.

What happens after a project is completed?

Traditionally, the answer is simple.

The project team moves on.

The report gets handed off.

Support tickets begin showing up.

Enhancement requests start accumulating.

Over time the solution slowly drifts away from the original business need.

This happens because projects have an end date.

Products do not.

A product mindset recognizes that business needs evolve continuously.

Customer expectations change.
Processes change.
Regulations change.
Markets change.

The data products supporting those business functions must evolve as well.

Instead of asking:

> How do we deliver this report?

Organizations begin asking:

> How do we continuously deliver value around this business capability?

That subtle shift changes everything.

# What Is a Data Product?

The term Data Product has become increasingly popular, but it is often misunderstood.

Some people think a data product is simply a dashboard.

Others believe it is a dataset.

Others use it interchangeably with a report.

The reality is that a data product is much broader.

A data product is a trusted, governed, reusable asset designed to solve a business problem.

It combines:

- data
- business logic
- governance
- quality controls
- ownership
- documentation
- user experience

Into a consumable product that delivers ongoing business value.

The key word is product.

Products have customers.

Products have owners.

Products evolve.

Products are measured.

Products solve problems.

A dashboard may be part of a data product.

But the dashboard itself is rarely the product.

# A Practical Example

Let's imagine a workers' compensation insurance company wants better visibility into claims performance.

Using a traditional reporting mindset, the request might look like this:

> Build me a dashboard showing claim counts, claim duration, and open claims by adjuster.

The dashboard gets built.

The request is considered complete.

Now let's look at the same problem through a data product lens.

The business problem is not actually the dashboard.

The business problem is improving claims management performance.

That changes the conversation.

Instead of delivering a dashboard, the team creates a Claims Performance Data Product.

The product may include:

- standardized claims metrics
- governed business definitions
- integrated claims and policy data
- operational dashboards
- executive scorecards
- predictive risk models
- AI-ready datasets
- data quality monitoring
- documentation and lineage

Now multiple groups can consume the same product.

Executives use scorecards.

Operations managers use dashboards.

Data scientists build predictive models.

Analysts perform ad-hoc analysis.

AI applications leverage the same trusted foundation.

One investment.

Multiple business outcomes.

That is the power of product thinking.

# Why Most Analytics Environments Become Chaotic

Many organizations unknowingly create reporting sprawl.

It usually starts innocently.

One department requests a dashboard.

Another department requests something similar.

A third department wants slightly different calculations.

Over time, the organization ends up with:

- hundreds of dashboards
- duplicate datasets
- conflicting KPIs
- inconsistent definitions
- redundant engineering effort

The business starts asking questions like:

> Why does Finance show one number while Operations shows another?

Or:

> Which dashboard should I trust?

These aren't technology problems.

They are product management problems.

Without ownership and governance, analytics environments naturally fragment.

Data product thinking introduces accountability.

Every product should have:

- a clear business purpose
- defined ownership
- identified consumers
- quality expectations
- measurable outcomes

Just like any other product within the organization.

# The Role of Governance in Data Products

One of the reasons data products have gained popularity is because they naturally align with governance.

Traditional governance efforts often struggle because governance is treated as a separate activity.

Data products embed governance directly into delivery.

Every data product should clearly answer:

- Who owns this product?
- What business problem does it solve?
- What are the approved metric definitions?
- Where does the data originate?
- How is quality measured?
- Who is authorized to access it?

When governance becomes part of the product lifecycle, it becomes significantly easier to maintain trust.

And trust is ultimately what drives adoption.

# Organizing Around Data Products

One of the most powerful changes organizations can make is aligning teams around business capabilities rather than technical functions.

Historically, work may flow through multiple teams:

Business Team → Business Analyst → Data Engineering → Reporting Team → QA → Production Support

Every handoff introduces delays.

Every handoff risks losing context.

Modern organizations increasingly organize around cross-functional data product teams.

A data product team may include:

- Product Owner
- Business Analyst
- Data Engineer
- Analytics Engineer
- BI Developer
- QA Analyst
- Data Steward
- Business Stakeholders

Together they own the entire lifecycle of the product.

This creates tighter alignment between business objectives and technical delivery.

Most importantly, it ensures accountability for outcomes rather than outputs.

# Measuring Success Differently

Traditional reporting teams often measure success through delivery metrics.

Examples include:

- reports created
- dashboards delivered
- stories completed
- tickets closed

Those metrics are easy to measure.

But they don't necessarily indicate business value.

Data products encourage different questions.

For example:

- Are people using the product?
- Has decision-making improved?
- Have manual processes been reduced?
- Has operational efficiency increased?
- Has data quality improved?
- Has adoption grown?

These metrics focus on outcomes rather than activity.

And ultimately, outcomes are what the business cares about.

# Data Products and AI

As organizations begin investing in AI, data products become even more important.

AI models require:

- trusted data
- standardized definitions
- quality controls
- governance
- reusable datasets

Organizations with mature data product ecosystems often accelerate AI adoption significantly faster than organizations operating with fragmented reporting environments.

Why?

Because the foundational assets already exist.

Instead of rebuilding datasets for every AI initiative, teams can leverage existing data products.

This reduces development time while improving consistency and trust.

In many ways, data products become the bridge between traditional analytics and [AI-enabled organizations](/ai/data/analytics/governance/lakehouse/2026/06/04/ai-enablement.html).

# Final Thoughts

Data product thinking is not about changing terminology.

It is about changing mindset.

The goal is no longer to build reports.

The goal is to create reusable, trusted, governed assets that continuously deliver business value.

Reports become one way of consuming those products.

Dashboards become one way of consuming those products.

AI becomes one way of consuming those products.

But the product itself remains the foundation.

Organizations that successfully make this shift often discover something interesting.

They spend less time building duplicate solutions.

Less time debating metrics.

Less time managing reporting sprawl.

And more time delivering meaningful business outcomes.

Because at the end of the day, businesses don't invest in dashboards.

They invest in solving problems.

And that is exactly what great data products are designed to do.
